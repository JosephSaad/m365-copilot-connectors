The rule that decides most of it

Own it → index it. Licence it → call it.

A Graph connector makes a persistent copy in a Microsoft-hosted index and grants it to Entra groups. Against your own content that is cheap, broad and correct. Against Moody's or S&P it collides with redistribution, per-seat entitlement, derived-data and AI-use clauses simultaneously — so licensed content is fetched live, entitled by the vendor, attributed and timestamped, and never stored.

Walk the tree

Eighteen questions that pick your route

The gates in Figure 1, plus the ones that decide it in practice more often than ownership does: which surface the answer has to appear in, whether the data may be resident in OneLake, whether the answer needs data joined across sources, how many people will ask and what that costs, how fast a deleted record has to disappear, and what infrastructure you actually have. Answer what you know — the first hard gate to fire wins, and the rest becomes watch-outs.

Q1 Where do the people asking sit?
Q2 Where must the answer actually appear?

The question that separates a Copilot connector from a Fabric data agent, and the one most often skipped. Neither Power BI Copilot nor a Fabric data agent grounds M365 Copilot chat the way indexed content does — reaching it from the Fabric side means publishing the data agent, either to the M365 Agent Store (preview) or as a tool to a Copilot Studio agent, and in both cases a person invokes it by name. Choosing Fabric to avoid a connector approval leaves the M365 grounding requirement unmet.

Q3 Who owns the data?
Q4 How is access controlled today?
Q5 What is the answer, actually?

Narrative density routes to indexed retrieval; numeric density routes to a semantic model; a write is a tool call by definition. Note the trap in the middle: descriptive metadata — which table holds what, who owns it, what feeds it — is a search problem over names, not an analytics one, so a catalogue is content even though everything it describes is numbers.

Q6 Is the data modelled for analytics?

This only changes the answer where the reason for calling live was a computed value or row-level security. A semantic model is built for both; it is not a document surface, so a catalogue of descriptive metadata still belongs in the index.

Q7 Can this data be resident in OneLake?

The question that picks the storage mode, and therefore where the rows physically sit.

Import is disqualified where no second copy is permitted, because it makes one. Direct Lake is disqualified where the data cannot be in OneLake, because that is where it reads from. What remains is DirectQuery — or a shortcut, which registers the data in OneLake without moving it and is the usual way out of that corner. Two constraints narrow it further: Direct Lake supports cloud connections only and cannot use a gateway of any kind, so nothing on-premises qualifies however it is shortcut; and the model workspace must sit in the same region as the data source workspace.

Q8 Does the answer need this data joined to other data?

The question that most often decides Fabric versus everything else, and the one least often asked.

A Graph index does not join. External items are flat and independent, so Copilot can rank across two connectors but cannot relate a row in one to a row in the other — and a tool call returns one source's answer, leaving the model to stitch, which it does plausibly rather than correctly. Joining is what a semantic model and OneLake are for: shortcuts register other sources in place, and one model can span a lakehouse and a warehouse through cross-database query.

Q9 How many people will ask — and can the source, and the budget, take it?

Two things turn on this, not one. On DirectQuery every visual becomes a native query at the source, per user, per interaction. And the economics invert with headcount: per-seat costs punish large populations, standing capacity punishes small ones.

Q10 How stale may an answer be?

Freshness is bounded by crawl interval on any index path. Nothing makes an index live.

Q11 Deletion SLA — how fast must a removed record stop appearing?

The question people forget. Nothing detects deletions on a direct push — removal is an API call your own code makes; a crawled connection detects them on its next pass.

Q12 What infrastructure do you actually have?
Q13 What can the source be read through?

The gallery now holds two kinds of connector and they behave oppositely. A synced connector crawls and indexes at org level under admin credentials. A federated connector indexes nothing: it fetches over MCP at question time, each user authenticating as themselves, and it is read-only. Note what a synced connector does not do for a partner-built or custom source: Microsoft has no visibility into deletions there, and its first sight of any change is your own call to the Graph API. Several market-data vendors publish federated connectors, which is the first configuration answer this page has ever been able to offer for licensed content. And you are not limited to the gallery: an organisation can register its own custom federated connector over an MCP server it hosts, which is how an internal source reaches M365 Copilot chat without being copied into the index.

Q14 Roughly how many items?
Q15 How many more sources like this are coming?
Q16 What does compliance require of the data boundary?
Q17 Does the model itself have to be governed?

This is model-risk governance, not data governance, and it is the reason a bank ends up hosting its own application when every data answer pointed at M365. No M365 surface lets you pin a model version or hold it still while you evaluate it.

Q18 Whose retrieval has to be good enough?

A synced connector gives you its retrieval, not yours. What you can change is the schema, the semantic labels and which content is in scope — not how a document is chunked, what embeds it, or how results are reranked. If retrieval quality is the thing being judged, that is an application you host.

      Delivery paths — how the data gets there

      The layer your original matrix doesn't cover, and the one where the money and the licensing risk actually sit. These are not alternatives to the surfaces above; every surface consumes one or more of them.

      Read the columns carefully — only some of them are alternatives to each other. Five axes are in play, and a column is mutually exclusive only with its own neighbours on the same one:

      • How the data reaches the model: pre-indexed retrieval (a synced connector, gallery or custom), a semantic model, or live invocation. This is the real fork, and it is what Figure 1 turns on. A semantic model is a third answer rather than a flavour of the other two: it can hold a copy like an index does, but it computes per user and enforces row-level security at query time like a live call does.
      • Which custom connector, once you have chosen to index: agent-hosted or direct push. Not a preference — your deletion SLA and your hosting decide it. Only the agent deletes, and only the push runs without a Windows host.
      • Which storage mode, once you have chosen a semantic model: Import, DirectQuery or Direct Lake. Also not a preference. Import copies rows into the model’s own store, so it dies wherever a second copy is forbidden. Direct Lake reads Delta Parquet out of OneLake, so it dies wherever the data cannot be resident there. DirectQuery stores nothing and survives both, at the price of latency and source load that scale with concurrency. A composite model mixes modes per table, so one table forcing a mode does not have to force the whole model. Direct Lake is itself two modes — on OneLake, which never falls back but does not apply the SQL endpoint’s row or column security, and on SQL, which honours it — falling back to DirectQuery for row-level rules, and erroring rather than leaking for object- and column-level ones.
      • How a live tool is exposed, once you have chosen invocation: a federated Copilot connector, an API action, an MCP server you build, or a ready-made MCP server somebody else publishes. Same job; different wire protocol, different client reach, and in the last case somebody else’s code between Copilot and your data. The federated connector is the interesting one, because it is an MCP server that Microsoft fronts: it collapses the old trade-off by reaching M365 Copilot chat with nothing copied and nothing wired per surface — at the cost of being read-only, limited to four experiences, connected by each user individually, and requiring a Copilot add-on licence for every one of them. It is also not gallery-only: an organisation can register its own MCP server as a custom federated connector, which is the route by which an internal source reaches Copilot chat without being indexed.
      • Where the whole thing runs: M365 surfaces, or an application you host. Foundry sits on this axis, not the other two — inside it you would still choose retrieval versus invocation, with your own index and your own tools. It is here because “the askers are not M365 users” is a real branch, not because it is a peer of a connector.

      One capability cuts across all five axes and belongs with them: joining. An index ranks but does not relate; a tool call returns one source; a semantic model exists to join. If the questions people will ask span sources, that fact outranks most of what follows — it is the argument for Fabric that survives when the residency and licensing arguments do not.

      What the columns genuinely share, and what the cost rows are really measuring, is reach per unit of build: a synced connector is picked up by every M365 surface with nothing told to it, a federated one by four of them with nothing built, an API action is wired to each agent by hand, an MCP server is wired per client over a shared protocol, and a semantic model reaches whoever already opens Power BI — and no further, until a data agent is published through Copilot Studio.

      A catalogue is not an analytics workload, and this is where people go wrong. Descriptive metadata — which table holds what, who owns it, what it is tagged with, what feeds it — is a search problem over names and descriptions, so it belongs in the index however structured it looks. A semantic model earns its place when the answer is a number computed across rows, or when row-level security has to be enforced per user. Those are different questions about the same warehouse, and they take different routes.

      The boundary people get wrong

      Two planes, and what belongs to each

      Grounding mechanisms are attributed to Fabric constantly, and most of them are not Fabric at all. The two planes have separate governance, separate funding and separate approval paths, and knowing which one you are standing in settles arguments that otherwise run for weeks.

      Microsoft 365 & Graph plane

      Retrieval — content is put where Copilot looks

      • Synced connectors crawl external content into the semantic index, at organisation level, under admin credentials. Gallery, custom agent-hosted, or direct push.
      • Federated connectors index nothing. They fetch over MCP at question time, each user authenticating as themselves, read-only, auditable in Purview — and you can register your own MCP server as one rather than waiting for a gallery entry.
      • Graph Connector Agent is the on-premises crawler for sources behind the firewall — a Windows host, with deletions detected on crawl like the gallery crawlers, rather than pushed by your own code.
      • Governed by the Microsoft 365 admin center and the connectors API. No Fabric capacity, no OneLake footprint, no F SKU.

      What it buys you: the answer appears in M365 Copilot chat next to every other connected source, with nothing wired per surface.

      Fabric plane

      Delegation — a query capability an agent calls

      • Semantic models in one of three storage modes, and Fabric data agents grounded on lakehouses, warehouses, KQL databases, mirrored databases and models — publishable to the M365 Agent Store or to Copilot Studio.
      • Fabric MCP servers — the local one is generally available; the remote Core, Data Warehouse and data-agent-as-MCP servers are previews. Check status before designing on them.
      • Governed by Fabric tenant settings and capacity admin, funded by the F SKU. Fabric REST APIs are a separate surface from Microsoft Graph.
      • Cross-geo caution, for the data-agent MCP endpoint specifically: it needs F2+ or P1+ with Fabric enabled, plus cross-geo processing and storage for AI switched on in tenant settings — and responses returned to an MCP client may leave Fabric’s compliance boundary and region, handled thereafter under the client’s own policies. Check each of the other Fabric MCP servers’ own docs before assuming the same terms.

      What it buys you: an answer computed over governed data, per user — but inside Fabric, until you publish the agent outward. Note that publishing it into Microsoft 365 moves the response out of Fabric’s compliance boundary and region, under M365’s terms thereafter.

      The distinction worth memorising

      Retrieval versus delegation.

      A connector pushes content into the index so Copilot retrieves it. Fabric MCP and a data agent expose a query capability an agent calls at runtime. The two are not substitutable, and the failure mode is always the same direction: a team picks Fabric to avoid a connector approval, ships it, and discovers the M365 Copilot grounding requirement was never met. Neither Copilot in Fabric nor a data agent makes anything retrievable in M365 Copilot chat the way indexed content is; a published data agent is something a person invokes by name, not grounding that arrives on its own. A federated connector is the one mechanism that sits in both frames at once — delegation by wire, retrieval by placement — which is exactly why it is the interesting new answer for content that may not be copied. Note where that lands the MCP server you were going to build anyway: registered as a custom federated connector, it stops being a thing wired per client and becomes a thing M365 Copilot chat reaches on its own.

      The split that matters most

      Data you own vs data you licence

      Same technology, opposite answers — and the deciding factor is contractual, not technical. Get the licence read before the architecture is drawn, because it changes the design rather than the paperwork.

      First-party — you own it

      Index it, and index it broadly

      • No redistribution question. It's yours; a second copy in your own tenant index costs you nothing contractual.
      • No per-query meter. Crawl cost is compute you already pay for. Widening the audience is free.
      • M365-wide reach for one build. One connector puts the content in Copilot chat, Word, Outlook, Teams and Search without a retrieval story per surface.
      • The second source is cheap. Once the engine exists, a new SQL source is a class and a config file — weeks becomes days.

      But not always. Row-level security, MNPI, restricted lists and dynamic information barriers are all first-party and all wrong for an index — a Graph ACL is written at push time, and a wall-crossing next Tuesday isn't in it. Those go through a live call like vendor data does.

      Third-party — you licence it

      Call it live, and never store it

      • Redistribution. A searchable corpus serving users is usually storage and redistribution, not internal use. It needs an explicit rider.
      • Entitlement counting. Index it, ACL it to a group, and you have entitled everyone in that group. Pay for 200 seats, expose 3,000, and that is exactly what an audit finds.
      • AI and derived-data clauses. Recent agreements address ingestion into AI systems explicitly. Several vendors now sell an AI-use tier — ask what it costs before designing around it.
      • Caching windows and attribution. Many agreements cap retention and require source and as-of timestamp. Last Tuesday's index entry satisfies neither.

      The trap: the LLM decides how many calls to make. "Compare our exposure across these fifteen counterparties" is fifteen vendor lookups per turn, per user. Model volume at 5–10× human usage, budget per user per day in the action itself, and log call volume from day one.

      Check the gallery first, though. Several market-data vendors now publish federated Copilot connectors — live fetch over MCP, nothing indexed, each user authenticating with their own entitlement. That is the shape this panel has always been arguing for, available as configuration rather than as a build, and it changes the seat-counting conversation from "everyone in the Entra group" to "everyone who connected it".

      The hybrid that resolves all three cases

      Index the metadata. Call for the value.

      Index that you hold a rating for an issuer, its as-of date, the methodology, your own analyst commentary, and a link — all of which is either yours or non-substantive. Then fetch the rating itself live, entitled and attributed. Copilot answers "which issuers did we downgrade internally last quarter, and what does Moody's say now?" — first half from the index, second from the call. Neither half alone gets there, and the licensed content never lands in the index. The same shape resolves a Power BI semantic model and a Fabric warehouse — index the measure definitions and ownership, and let a data agent or executeQueries compute the numbers, with row-level security still enforced where it lives.

      In practice

      Three questions, in order

      1. Where is the person standing when they ask — and where must the answer appear? Teams, Word or Outlook → an M365 surface, grounded by a connector. A report or a Fabric workspace → a semantic model and a data agent, which will not reach M365 chat without a Copilot Studio hop. Your public site or a call-centre desktop → Foundry or Copilot Studio; M365 Copilot is not reachable from there. An IDE or an analyst's tooling → MCP. These are two questions, not one, and answering only the first is how a connector gets built for a surface that does not read connectors.

      2. Is the answer content, or is it computed — and if it is computed, where may the rows sit? Text that can sit still — a ticket, a case note, a policy, a measure's definition — index it. A number that moves, or anything that writes — call it. Indexing a computed value freezes it, and a frozen number that disagrees with the live report is a control problem, not a nuisance. Once it is a model, residency picks the storage mode for you: in OneLake → Direct Lake, a copy permitted → Import, neither → DirectQuery. Then check the mode against question three, because Direct Lake on OneLake does not reproduce the source’s own row-level security.

      3. Who enforces access, and can they still do it afterwards? If the answer is "the source system, live, per user", you cannot index it without losing the enforcement. That single question resolves RLS, MNPI, information barriers and vendor seat licensing in one move — and it no longer costs you M365 reach, because a federated connector keeps enforcement at the source and still answers in Copilot chat.

      What only you can check

      Every claim on this page was checked against Microsoft documentation on 28 August 2026 — twice, the second time adversarially — and the cells were corrected in place rather than footnoted. What remains here is only what a document cannot settle for you: a licence position that changes under you, a reading instruction, a pricing policy, and a contract you hold.

      1. The licence row is the one most likely to change under you, and it is the one that decides whether a design is affordable rather than whether it works. Federated connectors in particular are restricted to tenants and users holding the Microsoft 365 Copilot add-on or E7 — they are explicitly not supported on a Copilot Studio licence or on Microsoft 365 Copilot pay-as-you-go. Confirm the entitlement of the actual user population, not the tenant, before pricing anything here.
      2. “The model stitches” is not a synonym for “joins”. Where a row says an answer is assembled by the model from separate calls or separate results, understand what that means: the model has no key, no cardinality and no way to know it dropped a record. For two or three facts a person could have reconciled by hand it is fine. For anything a SQL join would have done, it produces a plausible answer rather than a correct one, and the failure is silent. That is the distinction between the full and partial marks on this row, and it is the reason a genuinely integrated question belongs in a model.
      3. No prices anywhere on this page, deliberately. I cannot verify current M365 Copilot, Copilot Studio or Azure rate cards, and invented figures in a financial-services business case are worse than none. Effort bands and cost shape are what's stable; get the numbers from your licensing desk.
      4. Vendor licence terms are the gating item. The third-party column reflects the common shape of market-data agreements, not yours. Redistribution, derived data, caching, AI use and audit clauses need reading by your market-data licensing team, in writing, before engineering starts.
